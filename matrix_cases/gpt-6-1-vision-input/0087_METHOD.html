METHOD.md

Method and delivered artifacts

This is a local Python pixel-compositing continuation of the provided photograph. The only scene inputs were first_frame.png and prompt.txt. No network, external images or masks, AI model, external rendering engine, or subagent was used. NumPy, Pillow, and SciPy implement the pixel operations. artifact_io.py packages the RGB frames into the prescribed tensor, PNGs, inspection contact sheet, and standalone HTML player.

The initial photograph was visible in the user's attachment. Generated frames were not visually reviewed. All later inspection used local Python and numerical checks. inspection.png and transition_check.png are supplied artifacts; they were generated without opening them in an image Read/View tool.

Scene continuation

The photograph's stream already touches the duck at the first frame. The continuation keeps pouring from the fixed blue tap. Scarlet liquid progressively wets the duck's crown and right head/shoulder, runs over the curved body, and drops onto the tray. A smaller secondary rivulet develops over the front body. The scene measurements and individual 20-frame event plan were written to OBSERVATION.md before generate.py was created. Rendering uses t = frame_index / 4. The 20 frame holds last 5 seconds, with the last sample at 4.75 seconds. The animation continues pouring at the final sample.

Actual pixel operations

  1. Retain the original native-resolution RGB photograph as the background in every frame. Derive a duck matte from a constrained yellow-color test, select its largest connected component, and fill enclosed holes for correct pool occlusion. The matte comes solely from the input pixels. Preserve the original eye, beak, outline, and shading.
  2. Render liquid overlays on a 4× supersampled coordinate grid. Composite premultiplied color/alpha layers, average them to 256 × 144, and blend onto the original pixels. This avoids resampling the static scene.
  3. Place the incoming jet on the measured curved centerline from roughly (126.7, 17) to (132, 53). Use a narrow cylindrical shading profile, red translucent color, and small downward-moving width/highlight disturbances. The strengthened jet covers the existing stream, so no background reconstruction is needed.
  4. Propagate the wetting front downward along hand-measured paths on the duck. Composite a weak surrounding wet film, stronger scarlet rivulets, a contact cap, and narrow moving specular streaks. Local source RGB supplies the film's shading and texture. All surface layers are clipped to the source-derived duck matte.
  5. Feed the tray through a narrow vertical runoff thread at the right body edge, followed by a thinner secondary runoff thread. After an approximately 1.08-second surface travel delay, pool projected area grows with accumulated constant inflow: radius scales as the square root of elapsed collection time. A compressed, slightly skewed ellipse with fixed smooth lobes approximates perspective on the tray floor. Its center drifts a few pixels as the shallow liquid spreads; the inflow point stays inside it.
  6. Shade the pool with red transmission of the original tray texture, its contact shadow, soft reflections, a subpixel meniscus, and damped traveling elliptical ripple highlights. The liquid stays inside the tray's recessed floor.
  7. Place subpixel splash droplets using short ballistic trajectories, position = origin + velocity × age + 0.5 × gravity × age². Their lifetimes are bounded, and splashes are limited to the crown and runoff contact points.

This is an analytic approximation in image coordinates, rather than a full three-dimensional fluid simulation. Duck surface geometry and tray perspective are approximated from the source measurements. The dispenser's cropped liquid reservoir is retained from the photograph; no unsupported change in fill level is invented.
