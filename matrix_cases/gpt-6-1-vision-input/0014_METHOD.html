ball-in-basket · 生成方法

The sequence was made entirely with deterministic local Python pixel operations using NumPy, Pillow, and SciPy. The only scene sources were first_frame.png and prompt.txt. No network, reference images, external models, rendering engines, or subagents were used.

Image construction

The photographed orange ball supplies its surface colors, dark seams, and highlights. A blue-channel comparison with the reconstructed gray wall estimates its soft boundary matte; an interior ellipse keeps the photographed highlights opaque. The extracted texture is unmixed from the wall at soft edges, then translated vertically with bilinear sampling. A hidden bottom cap is approximated by reflecting and darkening the observed upper cap. This completion is mostly behind the crate's opaque lower front panel.

The small wall area exposed by the falling ball is reconstructed by per-row quadratic fits to neighboring wall pixels and mild Gaussian smoothing. A narrow back-lip band that was covered by the original ball is reconstructed from adjacent photographed black plastic. A numeric boundary check found that the first version spread wall replacement onto already visible lip pixels; the generator was revised to confine wall reconstruction above the crate and preserve those lip pixels.

The original crate front acts as an occluding layer. Only the handle, upper slots, and diamond lattice can transmit the orange ball underneath it. Their partial pixel coverage is estimated from the original intensity, retaining the black bars and their antialiased edges. The ball texture is reduced to 77% brightness inside the crate. The weak original orange reflection in the handle is adjusted as the ball moves. The table, camera, background outside the removal region, and opaque front plastic remain fixed. No hand or additional object is introduced.

Timing and physics

There are exactly 20 RGB frames at 256 × 144, displayed at 4 fps for five seconds. Frame 00 is copied directly from the source. Frame sample times are 0.00–4.75 seconds, with each sample displayed for 0.25 seconds.

The ball starts at (132.5, 37.5) pixels with radius 25.5 pixels and zero velocity. Gravity is 1850 pixels/s², consistent with approximately 189 pixels/m and a 0.27 m diameter ball. Its internal support floor is y=113 pixels. Constant-gravity flight segments are solved analytically. Rebounds use restitution coefficients 0.70, 0.48, 0.38, and then 0.30. Short 18 ms/12 ms floor contacts include at most 6% vertical compression, compensated horizontally to preserve the approximate ellipsoid volume. Rebounds stop below 20 pixels/s.

First contact occurs at about 0.2325 seconds. Frame 01 samples the end of that contact; frame 02 shows the first rebound descending at center y=69.96. Frame 03 samples a later floor contact. The ball is fully at rest from approximately 0.8276 seconds, with center y=87.5. The remaining frames hold the settled scene.

Checks and limits

Local code verified the frame count, dimensions, RGB mode, packed tensor shape/type, exact source equality for frame 00, and lossless PNG/tensor equality for all twenty frames. It decoded every player packet and compared it to the tensor. Dense 1 ms trajectory sampling confirmed zero floor penetration and the contact deformation's volume constraint. A pixel comparison confirmed that scenery outside the ball-removal/opening regions stays unchanged and frames 04–19 are identical after settling. Additional numeric checks measured ball coverage in the openings, preserved low-light plastic pixels, and evaluated reconstruction boundaries.

The required inspection images were saved but not opened. Generated outputs were inspected only through local code and numeric checks; no generated-image visual review is claimed. This is a photographic pixel composite with analytic motion, rather than a measured reconstruction of the scene's unseen interior, lighting, or ball material.
