OBSERVATION.md

Scene observation and event plan

The input is a 256 × 144 RGB photograph. The camera is fixed above a light wooden tabletop. The front edge of the table crosses the bottom of the frame; the yellow seating and dark background occupy the upper edge. The black pipe enters from the left at about y=54, and its mouth occupies x=13–21, y=40–67. A small grey ball is already outside the pipe in frame 00, centered approximately at (118.5, 52.5), with a radius of about 8 pixels. Its soft shadow extends mostly toward image-left, with a small contact shadow below it. The heavy black kettlebell occupies roughly x=175–217, y=0–55, with its lower left collision surface around (179, 44). The yellow duck is at about (225, 39), radius 10 pixels. The ball, pipe and collision point define a nearly horizontal tabletop path that slopes slightly upward in image coordinates toward the right.

Only the ball moves. The initial frame already shows the launch in progress, so the continuation begins from that exact visible position. The ball reaches the kettlebell at 0.25 s, recoils leftward, and slows on the table. A 30 lb kettlebell has far more mass than a tennis ball: it remains visibly fixed, as does the duck. There are no added objects, cuts, lighting changes or camera movements.

The continuation will use local pixel compositing: reconstruct the small exposed tabletop patch, extract the ball and its soft shading from the source, translate it along a straight table-plane path, and render a moving contact shadow. At contact the ball will be mildly compressed. A rolling seam may rotate subtly while preserving the source ball's appearance. Exposure blur will be modest and limited to the moving ball. All other source pixels remain fixed outside the small ball cleanup region and moving ball/shadow footprint.

Refinements after pixel inspection

The generated ball uses its existing source texture and highlight; no added seam or rotating illumination was necessary at this resolution. The tabletop restoration uses a same-frame grain donor displaced by (-43, +4) pixels and local Poisson blending. Ball centers are computed continuously using 92 pixels/s initial rebound speed and 44 pixels/s² deceleration, so the actual stopping center is (75.86, 58.84) at about 2.34 s. The collision frame uses 9% horizontal compression. The source kettlebell, duck and pipe pixels are protected from the moving ball and shadow. The contact and rebound sheet was inspected with an image-view tool, and the initially observed shadow overlap on the kettlebell was corrected.

METHOD.md

Generation method

This is a deterministic continuation made with local Python pixel operations using NumPy, Pillow and OpenCV. The only scene inputs are first_frame.png and prompt.txt. No network, reference imagery, learned model, external renderer, external physics engine or subagent was used.

The background is the initial photograph. The original ball and its shadow are removed from a small elliptical region by copying nearby tabletop grain from the same photograph, then blending that donor with OpenCV's local Poisson image operation. The source image supplies the grey ball's color, blurred edge, surface texture and lighting. A soft elliptical alpha and edge decontamination against the reconstructed table allow subpixel translation without transporting a visible table rectangle. The ball's lighting remains fixed with respect to the camera. No visible rolling seam was added.

The ball follows the straight image-plane line from (118.5, 52.5) to (171.0, 44.7) during the first 0.25 seconds. This continues the launch already shown in the input. Frame 01 samples contact with the kettlebell, with 9% horizontal ball compression and compensating vertical expansion. After contact, the ball rebounds at 92 image pixels per second and slows at 44 image pixels per second squared. It stops at approximately (75.86, 58.84) after 2.34 seconds. These are chosen screen-space kinematics for the demonstration, rather than a measured simulation of the physical setup. The much heavier kettlebell remains visibly stationary.

The moving ball has an analytic broad shadow extending to image-left and a small contact shadow. A three-sample subpixel exposure kernel contributes at most 0.7 pixels of motion softness. Source pixels belonging to the kettle, duck and pipe are preserved to prevent shadow or sprite overlap from altering them. There is no camera motion, crop, scene cut or lighting animation. The final resting pose is held through frame 19.

generate.py produces the 20 RGB frames at 256 × 144, corresponding to four frames per second and five seconds including the last frame's quarter-second display interval. Frame 00 is copied directly from the input, without a cleanup or rendering pass. artifact_io.py writes all PNGs, the standalone index.html player, the inspection sheet, and pixels.npz. The tensor key is pixels, its shape is (144, 256, 20), and its dtype is uint32. Each value is packed as 0xRRGGBB.

inspection.png shows selected frames. transition_check.png shows consecutive early frames, enlarged source-pixel contact crops, and amplified adjacent-frame differences. Both generated sheets were opened with the image-view tool; the final revised transition sheet was reviewed after protecting the apparatus from shadow overlap. Inspection supports the visible contact, leftward rebound, stopping ball, fixed scene and clean restoration of the original ball position.

Automated verification checks all 20 decoded PNGs against their packed tensor slices and the generated arrays, checks frame 00 against the input exactly, and checks the static region and protected apparatus against the source. The results and exact generated ball centers are recorded in verification.json. The HTML delta stream is also decoded and compared with the tensor to verify that the player contains the same frames.
